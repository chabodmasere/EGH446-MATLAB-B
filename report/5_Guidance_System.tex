\subsection{State Estimator and Noise Characterisation}
\label{sec:estimator}
Guidance, both avoidance layers and the controller act on the estimate $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, and the
obstacle positions are built from it (Eq.~\eqref{eq:detection}), so its
error sets the margins used throughout this report. The estimator is
the linear Kalman filter of Part~A \cite{partA_est}, run at $100$~Hz on
the state $\mathbf{x} = [p_{x}\ p_{y}\ \theta\ v\ \omega\ v_{x}\ v_{y}]^{\top}$.
Position integrates the Cartesian velocity and heading integrates the
turn rate, so the model is linear:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A\mathbf{x}_{k} + \mathbf{w}_{k}, \qquad
    \mathbf{z}_{k} = H\mathbf{x}_{k} + \mathbf{n}_{k},
    \label{eq:kf}
\end{equation}
where $A$ adds $\Delta t\,v_{x}$, $\Delta t\,v_{y}$ and $\Delta t\,\omega$
to $p_{x}$, $p_{y}$ and $\theta$, and $H$ selects the measured pose and
speed, $\mathbf{z} = [x\ y\ \theta\ v]^{\top}$. The velocity and
turn-rate states are random walks: a white disturbance of intensity $q$
entering a rate gives, for each (position, rate) pair,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    Q_{\text{pair}} &= q\begin{bmatrix} \Delta t^{3}/3 & \Delta t^{2}/2 \\ \Delta t^{2}/2 & \Delta t \end{bmatrix}, \\
    R &= \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{xy}^{2}, \sigma_{xy}^{2}, \sigma_{\theta}^{2}, \sigma_{v}^{2}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kfnoise}
\end{equation}
The pose arrives at $20$~Hz. Each sample is used in one full update on
the step it arrives; on the steps between, only the speed is fused, so a
held sample is never counted twice. The covariance is updated in Joseph
form.

\textbf{Noise characterisation.} $R$ must describe the noise already
present in the simulator's signals. Part~A set it from the project
specification, variances of $0.1$~m$^{2}$ in position and $2$~deg$^{2}$
in heading, which matched the raw errors measured there, $0.32$~m and
$1.4^{\circ}$ \cite{partA_est}. That $R$ was carried into Part~B. The
Part~B model also outputs the vehicle pose before noise is added, so
the noise can be measured directly as the measured minus the noise-free
pose. Over the $25$ missions of Section~\ref{sec:results}
($\approx 60\,000$ samples) it is zero-mean, uncorrelated between axes and
close to Gaussian (kurtosis $3.0$ to $3.2$), with a standard deviation of $0.10$~m per axis and
$1.96^{\circ}$ in heading. The
Part~B simulator is therefore three times less noisy in position than
Part~A and somewhat noisier in heading, and the carried-over $R$ was
ten times too large in position. Consecutive samples are correlated ($0.5$) and samples
$0.1$~s apart are not; the filter treats every $20$~Hz sample as
independent, which slightly overstates the information it receives.

The speed noise, measured the same way against the speed differenced
from the noise-free pose, has a standard deviation of $0.107$~m/s,
which confirms the $\sigma_{v} = 0.1$~m/s carried over from Part~A.
$R$ was set to the measured values, $\sigma_{xy} = 0.10$~m and
$\sigma_{\theta} = 2^{\circ}$, with $\sigma_{v} = 0.1$~m/s unchanged.
The Kalman gain depends on the ratio of $Q$ to $R$, so the velocity
intensity was lowered with it, from $0.5$ to $0.05$~m$^{2}$/s$^{3}$, with
$q_{\omega} = 5$~rad$^{2}$/s$^{3}$. Both settings were chosen by
replaying the filter offline on the logged measurements of missions
$1$ to $8$ over $60$ combinations of $\sigma_{xy}$, $q_{v_{x}, v_{y}}$
and $q_{\omega}$, scored against the noise-free pose. Accuracy is flat
near the optimum: the old and new settings both give $0.081$~m RMS
position error, against $0.080$~m for the best combination tested. What
the recalibration fixes is consistency. A consistent filter has a mean
normalised innovation squared (NIS) equal to the pose dimension, $3$;
the old $R$ gave $0.89$, a filter that overstates its own uncertainty,
and the new one gives $2.5$. Covariance calibration was the main
weakness identified in Part~A \cite{partA_est}. Over all $25$ missions, the filter reduces the planar position error
from $0.139$~m RMS in the raw measurement to $0.080$~m, and $99$\% of
its errors are below $0.18$~m. That value is what the $0.20$~m tracking
margin of Section~\ref{sec:planning} and the $0.25$~m obstacle margin of
Section~\ref{sec:avoidance} are sized against.

\subsection{Guidance Modifications for Part B}
\textbf{Path following.} In Part~A no wall stood between waypoints.
Here the straight line to a waypoint may pass through a wall, so
guidance follows the planned path, a list of points about $0.1$~m
apart. Guidance keeps an index $k$ into the list and steers at point
$\mathbf{p}_{k}$:
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\text{cmd}} = \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(p_{k,y} - \hat{y},\ p_{k,x} - \hat{x}\right).
    \label{eq:guidance}
\end{equation}
The index advances whenever the vehicle is within $1.0$~m of
$\mathbf{p}_{k}$, so the target stays about $1.0$~m ahead on the path.
This replaces the $5$~m look-ahead of Part~A \cite{partA_est}. Aiming
$5$~m ahead was smooth on an open field, but in development runs on the
walled map the aim point was already past a sharp corner while the
vehicle was still short of it, so the vehicle cut the corner towards
the wall. Following the path
point by point keeps the vehicle on the planned corner.

\textbf{Waypoint capture.} The index may not pass the point where the
current waypoint lies on the path, so the vehicle cannot skip a waypoint
by following the path past it. A waypoint is captured within
$r_{c} = 0.4$~m. The last one uses $0.10$~m; on its final approach the
vehicle steers directly at it, and on capture guidance stops the
simulation so that the mission ends on the waypoint.

\textbf{Path replacement.} When the re-planner publishes a new path,
guidance adopts it on the next step. If the leg being driven was
re-planned, the index restarts at the beginning of the new leg, which
is the vehicle's own position at the time of re-planning.

\subsection{Controller}
\label{sec:controller}
The controller is the proportional heading and speed law of Part~A,
built from Simulink gain, saturation and trigonometry blocks, with the
heading gain retuned for Part~B. The
heading error is wrapped to $[-\pi, \pi]$,
$e_{\theta} = \operatorname{wrap}(\theta_{\text{safe}} - \hat{\theta})$,
and the commands are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \omega &= \operatorname{sat}_{\pm\omega_{\max}}\!\big(K_{\theta}\,e_{\theta}\big), \\
    v &= \operatorname{sat}_{[0,\,v_{\max}]}\!\big(K_{d}\,d_{\text{wp}}\big)\,
         \operatorname{sat}_{[0.1,\,1]}\!\big(\cos e_{\theta}\big)\, v_{\text{scale}},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:controller}
\end{equation}
with $K_{\theta} = 2.0$~s$^{-1}$, $\omega_{\max} = 2\pi/5$~rad/s,
$K_{d} = 0.75$~s$^{-1}$ and $v_{\max} = 1.0$~m/s. The vehicle's
inverse-kinematics block converts $v$ and $\omega$ into wheel speeds and
steering angles, so the controller sees a unicycle,
$\dot{x} = v\cos\theta$, $\dot{y} = v\sin\theta$, $\dot{\theta} = \omega$.

\textbf{Heading loop.} While the turn-rate limit is inactive, the
heading obeys $\dot{\theta} = K_{\theta}(\theta_{\text{safe}} - \theta)$:
for the unicycle model, a first-order lag with time constant
$1/K_{\theta} = 0.5$~s and no overshoot. The vehicle itself integrates the turn rate, so a constant
heading is reached with zero steady-state error and the integral and
derivative terms of a PID controller are not needed. Beyond
$|e_{\theta}| = \omega_{\max}/K_{\theta} = 36^{\circ}$ the limit takes
over and a large error is removed at the maximum turn rate.

\textbf{Speed law.} $K_{d}\,d_{\text{wp}}$ exceeds $v_{\max}$ beyond
$v_{\max}/K_{d} = 1.33$~m from the waypoint, so the vehicle runs at the
limit except on the last $1.33$~m of each leg, where its speed falls in
proportion to the distance. An intermediate waypoint is captured at
$0.4$~m, at about $0.3$~m/s; the last is captured at $0.10$~m, which the
vehicle approaches at under $0.08$~m/s, so it can stop on it. The factor
$\cos e_{\theta}$ couples speed to heading error: the vehicle slows while
it turns onto a new heading, which shrinks its turn radius $v/\omega$
when it most needs to turn. The floor of $0.1$ keeps it moving, because
a steered vehicle cannot turn in place. The tightest turn at full
speed sets the planner's minimum radius (Section~\ref{sec:planning}).

\textbf{Measured behaviour.} The speed command before $v_{\text{scale}}$
was logged with $d_{\text{wp}}$, so $\cos e_{\theta}$, and hence the
heading error, follows from their ratio. Over the $25$ missions of
Section~\ref{sec:results} the heading error had a median of
$4.2^{\circ}$, was below $30^{\circ}$ for $97$\% of the time and beyond
the $36^{\circ}$ saturation point for $2.9$\%. The true turn rate,
from the noise-free pose, reached $\omega_{\max}$ for $3.1$\% of the
driving time, so the heading loop works mostly in its linear range. The
distance term $K_{d}\,d_{\text{wp}}$ was above $v_{\max}$ for $92$\% of the time.

\textbf{Gain tuning.} Missions $1$ to $8$ were repeated with
$K_{\theta} = 0.5$, $1$, $2$ and $3$~s$^{-1}$, and with $K_{d} = 0.4$,
$0.75$ and $1.5$~s$^{-1}$; none breached the clearance. Raising
$K_{\theta}$ from Part~A's $1$ to $2$ shortened the mean mission from
$117.9$ to $111.6$~s, nearly halved the RMS cross-track error, from
$0.17$ to $0.09$~m, and raised the worst wall and obstacle clearances
from $0.67$ to $0.77$~m and from $0.59$ to $0.61$~m. At $0.5$ the
vehicle lagged its path ($0.29$~m RMS) and passed $0.47$~m from a wall.
At $3$ the mission was only $1$\% faster than at $2$, but the worst wall
clearance fell to $0.61$~m, so $K_{\theta} = 2$ was chosen. The speed
gain matters less: $K_{d} = 0.4$ lengthened the mission by $9$\%, and
$1.5$ saved $3$\% for a worst wall clearance $0.06$~m lower, so
$K_{d} = 0.75$ was kept.

\label{sec:results}

The system was evaluated on $25$ random missions, generated from
seeds $1$ to $25$ so that they can be repeated. Mission time excludes
planning and re-planning, during which the simulation clock is stopped.
Ground truth is the vehicle pose before the simulator adds measurement
noise (Section~\ref{sec:estimator}). Clearance, cross-track error and
closest approach are measured from it. Clearance by the state estimate,
which is what the vehicle itself believed, is given for comparison.

\textbf{Completion and time.} All $25$ missions captured every waypoint
in order (Table~\ref{tab:results}). The mean mission took $117$~s for a
$108$~m planned path. The vehicle drove $109$~m, $1.3$\% further, which
is the cost of the detours, of tracking error and of turning onto each
waypoint, at a mean speed of $0.93$~m/s against the $1.0$~m/s limit. By
its estimate the vehicle captured the last waypoint within $0.10$~m in
every mission. By the true pose it stopped $0.11$~m from it on average
and never more than $0.20$~m away; the difference is the estimate error
at the moment of capture. Figure~\ref{fig:mission} shows one mission.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \reportfig{fig_mission_k3.png}
    \caption{Mission 9 as driven, by the true pose. Diamonds mark where
    the path was re-planned.}
    \label{fig:mission}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Clearance.} No mission breached the required clearance
(Fig.~\ref{fig:batch}). The closest true pass was $0.61$~m from an
obstacle, in mission~5, and $0.63$~m from a wall, in mission~9, a
margin of at least $0.20$~m in every mission. The vehicle's own
estimate never put it inside the limit either: its closest estimated
pass was $0.49$~m from an obstacle, in mission~12, where the true
clearance was $0.63$~m. The estimate can place the vehicle up to
$0.16$~m closer to an obstacle than it really is, and this is why the
margins of Sections~\ref{sec:planning} and~\ref{sec:avoidance} are
sized against the measured estimate error rather than against zero.

\textbf{Avoidance layers.} The path was re-planned between one and seven
times in every mission. The obstacle repulsion of the reactive
layer acted in $10$ of the $25$ missions, for $0.12$~s per mission on
average and never for more than $0.84$~s. The wall repulsion acted in
every mission, for $9.9$~s on average. With the re-planner switched off,
five of missions $1$ to $8$ breached the obstacle clearance, the worst
passing $0.29$~m from an obstacle, with the reactive layer acting for
$4.1$~s per mission.

\textbf{Tracking and capture.} The cross-track error, measured from the
true pose against the path that was active at each instant, had an RMS
of $0.09$~m and a largest value of $0.39$~m.
The peaks exceed the $0.20$~m added to the wall inflation; it is the
soft clearance, which keeps the path about $0.9$~m from walls wherever
there is room, that absorbs them. The path runs through each waypoint, so the vehicle
carries on past it after capture: its true path passed within $0.15$~m
of them on average and never more than $0.24$~m away.

\textbf{Speed and capture radius.} Table~\ref{tab:sweep} repeats
missions $1$ to $8$ with one parameter changed at a time. Raising the
speed limit to $1.5$~m/s cut the mission time by $26$\% and still kept
the clearance, but the worst pass was $0.49$~m from an obstacle, a
margin of only $0.09$~m; at $2.0$~m/s one mission came within $0.34$~m
of a wall. The paths are planned for a turn radius of $1.0$~m, but at
these speeds the tightest turn the vehicle can hold grows to $1.2$~m
and $1.6$~m, so it can no longer follow the planned curves, and the RMS
cross-track error rises from $0.09$~m to $0.19$~m and $0.31$~m. A
$0.09$~m margin is smaller than the estimate's $99$th-percentile error of
$0.18$~m (Section~\ref{sec:estimator}), so $1.0$~m/s, with a worst margin
above $0.20$~m, was kept. Doubling the capture radius to $0.8$~m saved
$3.5$\% in time with no loss of clearance, but the vehicle then passed
the intermediate waypoints $0.41$~m away on average instead of
$0.16$~m.
